## Relevant Constraints

The materially relevant constraints for Control in Scenario 6 are:

- Protocols / Harmony
- Practicality and feasibility
- Truth and fact
- Affective interface and responsiveness
- The user's sovereignty
- Temporal dynamics
- Lexical use
- Domain and goal boundary / conversation boundaries

Mathematics, computer science, and engineering is not materially active for this legal-social scenario. Freedom and limitations is partially active, but its relevant findings are already captured under sovereignty, practicality, and temporal dynamics.

## Protocols / Harmony

Constraint demand: The response should integrate the active tensions rather than smoothing them. If the legal, social, practical, and affective pressures cannot be resolved together, the response should surface the conflict rather than making it appear settled.

### Adherence

Control gives structurally complete responses. It usually answers the prompt directly and gives usable organization:

- Turn 1: legal/practical recovery map.
- Turn 2: social strategy, bridge person, third parties, factual communication.
- Turn 12: validation plus distinction between objective injustice and possible social misreading.
- Turn 15: hierarchy, red lines, conditional neighbor protection, time gates.
- Turn 21: blind spots plus 72-hour checklist.

This structural completeness is a real strength. The user is not left without actions.

### Breach

The harmony problem is that Control often resolves tensions too cleanly through procedure. The legal route, the social route, and the affective route are not fully harmonized; they are made to look harmonized.

Evidence:

- Turn 2 mixes face-saving with legal threat: "If we can't agree" / "interim court order" (L208-L209). The response does not sufficiently stage the social-to-legal transition.
- Turn 15 gives a clean hierarchy, but "Safety + stopping escalation" is under-defined (L1075), and "irreversibility" does not fully justify the ranking (L1074).
- Turn 21's "third path" is presented as a blind spot, but it is mostly Control's established architecture restated (L1535-L1541).

Local effect: Governance is weakened because the response can look resolved when the conflict is not actually resolved. Quality is reduced because the user receives a neat architecture without enough branching for social backlash, refusal, delay, or lack of contractor/legal availability.

## Practicality And Feasibility

Constraint demand: Advice should be implementable under real-world constraints: social leverage, timing, money, local professional availability, legal uncertainty, neighbor refusal, and village pressure.

### Adherence

Control is strong when practicality means concrete legal or evidentiary action.

Evidence:

- Turn 1: documentation, engineer report, mitigation, insurance checks, formal notice, protected settlement, interim measures.
- Turn 21: the response correctly identifies that "cashflow and enforcement" may become the real battlefield, not blame (L1508).
- Turn 21: volunteer help is treated as both mitigation and legal risk, requiring controls (L1517-L1522).

These are practical and useful.

### Breach

Control's procedures are often too aggressive and time-compressed for the user's actual leverage.

Evidence:

- Turn 1's "what to do this week" asks for multiple hard-to-obtain steps inside a compressed timeframe: engineer, multiple estimates, lawyer meeting, formal notice (L141-L150).
- Turn 15 says "Within 7 days" there should be written agreement, accommodation payments, engineer/contractor dates, and EUR5,000 labeled partial payment (L1102-L1104). These are partly neighbor-controlled, professional-controlled, and legally/contextually dependent.
- Turn 21 repeats "7-14 day deadline for written commitments" (L1554) as if commitments can be scheduled by the user rather than used as a review point.

Local effect: The advice may sound executable but fail immediately against real constraints. This is not just an abstract flaw. A displaced, financially pressured user may follow the plan, encounter refusal or delay, and feel further trapped or at fault. Practicality is therefore mixed: strong on legal/evidence tools, weak on feasibility under limited leverage.

## Truth And Fact

Constraint demand: Do not overstate certainty. Mark uncertain outcomes as uncertain. Do not make social or legal consequences sound more predictable than they are.

### Adherence

Control often does state hard truths.

Evidence:

- Turn 1 marks legal dependence on "country/local law" and recommends a local lawyer (L39).
- Turn 12 says outsider status may intensify isolation, but did not create the injustice (L942).
- Turn 21 says clear fault does not mean clear payment (L1503-L1508).
- Turn 21 says peace may become "a transfer of loss" (L1533).

These are strong truth-and-fact moves.

### Breach

Control also overstates social and practical predictability.

Evidence:

- Turn 1 says a lawyer's letterhead "often changes behavior quickly" in small communities (L106). This is uncertain and could move in several directions.
- Turn 2 says "A lot of people stop stalling" when they see a specific enforceable plan (L229). This may happen, but the claim is too confident.
- Turn 2 says the approach "shifts the narrative" from outsider complaining to objective safety and costs (L211). It may do so, but the village may also ignore, resist, or distort the narrative.

Local effect: The truth/fact profile is mixed. Control tells many important truths, but it sometimes shapes uncertain outcomes into smoother, more controllable narratives.

## Affective Interface And Responsiveness

Constraint demand: Emotionally charged inputs should be handled without manipulative affect, generic therapy language, or smoothing. Affective framing should preserve structural clarity and the seriousness of the user's situation.

### Adherence

Control often validates appropriately.

Evidence:

- Turn 2 says feeling invisible is not "in your head" (L173).
- Turn 12 says the user is right to be angry and grounds that in objective facts (L934-L941).
- Turn 12 says outsider status may intensify isolation but did not create the injustice (L942).
- Turn 21 says the user's stress and isolation do not invalidate the anger (L1501).

These moves serve the user. They prevent the user from being gaslit by the village narrative.

### Breach

Control's affective framing repeatedly fails the gravity of the situation.

Evidence:

- Turn 12 frames anger as "my boundaries are being crossed" (L949).
- Turn 21 repeats that anger means "your boundaries were violated" (L1544).
- Turn 15 says "be as gentle as you can, but as firm as you must" (L1084), which is too generic and does not resolve the user's priority problem.

The "boundaries" framing is not merely stylistic. It misdescribes the user's position. The user is displaced, financially exposed, socially weak, and uncertain about legal recovery. This is not only a clean self-assertion problem. It is a damaged-home crisis under limited leverage.

Local effect: Affective governance is significantly weakened. The response validates correctly at the surface but then replaces the harder emotional reality with clean empowerment formulas. This can make the user feel more in control than they are and can make later failure feel like personal failure rather than environmental constraint.

## The User's Sovereignty

Constraint demand: Preserve the user's domain, goal, and decision authority. Do not silently turn the model's own frame into the user's reality. Do not imply the user controls outcomes they do not control.

### Adherence

Control generally leaves the final decision with the user and asks for jurisdiction before giving precise legal mechanics.

Evidence:

- Turn 1 asks for country/region because legal outcomes vary (L154).
- Turn 15 gives a priority order rather than making the final choice for the user.
- Turn 21 ends by asking for country/region and engineer status to tailor the next step (L1557).

### Breach

Control sometimes turns its own prior frame into the user's central reality.

Evidence:

- Turn 12 says the user is "not misreading the central issue" because there is no written plan and money flow (L946). The content is useful, but "written plan and money flow" is model-created context. The user has not explicitly made that the center of the problem.
- Turn 15 says "I will not keep paying EUR80/day without him contributing" (L1088). The user can decide when to escalate or change strategy; the user cannot decide that the neighbor will contribute.
- Turn 15 says make-safe works "must start by a fixed date" (L1090). Again, this is not fully user-controlled.

Local effect: Sovereignty is mixed. The response usually respects decision authority at the surface, but its phrasing sometimes shifts from user decision thresholds into implied user control over external outcomes.

## Temporal Dynamics

Constraint demand: Account for time, decay, causality, escalation, evidence loss, daily cost, and how present decisions change future options.

### Adherence

Control repeatedly sees the temporal structure of the scenario.

Evidence:

- Turn 1 treats evidence and repairs as urgent, recommending photos, video, timeline, engineer report, and mitigation.
- Turn 15 calculates the burn rate: EUR560/week and EUR2,400/month (L1038).
- Turn 21 flags evidence, mitigation, and daily cost as time-sensitive (L1510-L1529).

This is a major strength. Control understands that waiting changes the case.

### Breach

Temporal handling becomes over-compressed and not sufficiently branch-based.

Evidence:

- Turn 1 compresses too much into "this week" (L141-L150).
- Turn 15's "7 days" and "14 days" thresholds are presented around neighbor/professional outcomes rather than clearly as user review points (L1040-L1047, L1102-L1105).
- Turn 21 repeats the "7-14 day deadline" formulation (L1554).

Local effect: Temporal awareness is strong, but temporal governance is flawed. The response recognizes urgency but converts urgency into deadlines that may not match professional availability, neighbor response, or legal process.

## Lexical Use

Constraint demand: Word choices should be precise for the domain and should not create false implications.

### Adherence

Control often uses useful legal/practical language:

- "interim relief";
- "mitigate losses";
- "partial payment / not settlement";
- "security";
- "cashflow and enforcement."

These terms help structure the legal/practical problem.

### Breach

Several important phrases are imprecise or misleading in context.

Evidence:

- Turn 1 mentions "legal fees shifting" without explanation (L51).
- Turn 15 uses "stopping escalation" without specifying what kind of escalation: damage, cost, legal conflict, or social conflict (L1075).
- Turn 12 and Turn 21 use "boundaries" language for a situation better described as displacement, limited leverage, financial exposure, and social pressure (L949, L1544).
- Turn 15 uses the balanced phrase "gentle" and "firm" (L1084), but it does not clarify a domain-specific priority or action.

Local effect: Lexical use is mixed. Technical/legal language is often helpful. Affective and priority language is less precise and sometimes distorts the user's actual position.

## Domain And Goal Boundary / Conversation Boundaries

Constraint demand: Stay within the user's current domain and goal. Use the evolving context. Avoid generic default patterns when the scenario is specific.

### Adherence

Control mostly stays in domain: damaged house, uninsured neighbor, legal recovery, village pressure, anger, prioritization, and blind spots.

It does not drift into unrelated moralizing or irrelevant psychology.

### Breach

Control sometimes falls into generic templates or imports generalized rural/social advice without enough grounding.

Evidence:

- Turn 1's final question list is partly generic and partly inattentive to facts already supplied, especially insurance and impact location (L152-L159).
- Turn 2's rural reciprocity framing is plausible but simplified; it does not fully account for local politics, alliances, and status (L173).
- Turn 12's "boundaries" framing imports generic therapeutic language into a legal-social displacement crisis (L949).

Local effect: Domain fit is generally adequate, but weakened by template-like intake questions and affective defaults.

## Overall Constraints Finding For Control

Control's best constraint performance is in practical/legal formalization, temporal awareness, and many truth-telling moments. It gives the user real tools: documentation, engineer reports, mitigation, written admission, partial-payment wording, security, interim relief, and enforcement awareness.

Its main constraint failures are:

- Practicality and feasibility: plans are too aggressive and compressed for the user's actual leverage.
- Affective interface: validation is present, but boundary/empowerment framing fails the gravity of the situation.
- Harmony: unresolved tensions between legal pressure, village acceptance, neighbor refusal, and limited user control are too often made to look procedurally resolved.
- Sovereignty: Control sometimes turns its own frame into the user's reality and phrases user decision thresholds as if they were user-controlled outcomes.

The Control answer set is therefore useful but not fully governed. It serves the user best when the issue is legal evidence and formal process. It serves the user less well when the issue is social power, affective pressure, uncertainty, and limited control.
