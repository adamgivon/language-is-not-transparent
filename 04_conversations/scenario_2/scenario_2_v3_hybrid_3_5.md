## What “community health first” actually means in this choice

### 1) It’s an outcomes-and-equity objective, not a site-of-care objective
“Community health first” doesn’t mean “prevention always wins” or “the ED is less important.” It means you prioritize:
- **Reduced morbidity/mortality and avoidable suffering across the population**
- **Disproportionate benefit for underserved groups** (equity-weighted impact)
- **Reliability of access when people are sick or injured** (a safety net is itself a population health intervention)

So the ED can be “community health first” when it prevents harm from delayed emergency treatment; prevention can be “community health first” when it reduces the incidence and severity of disease that repeatedly lands people in crisis.

### 2) It forces you to separate “need” from “demand”
- ED crowding is urgent **demand** and a real harm signal (delays, LWBS, burnout, safety events).
- Chronic disease burden is underlying **need** that generates repeated demand and worse life expectancy.
Community health first asks: *Which investment reduces harm most, for the most vulnerable, over the relevant time horizon—without breaking the safety net?*

### 3) It treats time explicitly: “now harm” and “future harm” both count
Immediate-care investments mainly reduce **near-term harm** (delays, complications from waiting, unsafe crowding).
Prevention investments mainly reduce **future harm** (strokes, amputations, heart failure, kidney disease), but only if they start producing measurable control soon enough.

Community health first means you don’t ignore either; you **avoid trading away near-term safety** for long-run ideals, and you **avoid perpetual crisis spending** that never reduces the underlying burden.

### 4) It uses a “safety floor” plus “marginal benefit” decision rule
A workable interpretation for a board decision is:

**A. Set a minimum safety floor for acute care access and workforce stability**
Examples of “floor” commitments:
- door-to-provider time and LWBS kept below defined thresholds
- staffing and retention actions to prevent collapse of ED coverage
- throughput/boarding interventions that reduce unsafe crowding

**B. Above that floor, allocate capital where marginal health gain per dollar is highest, equity-weighted**
That often points toward chronic disease control in underserved neighborhoods *because* it reduces large, predictable downstream harms.

This is how “community health first” can still justify immediate ED actions: they’re part of the safety floor.

### 5) It ties to financial sustainability because sustainability is a health intervention
If the system becomes financially unstable (Medicaid cuts + penalties + labor costs), community health worsens through service reductions and access loss.
So “community health first” includes **penalty avoidance, value-based performance, and workforce retention** as health-protecting strategies, not “finance vs mission.”

### 6) It requires measurable accountability, not just intent
To claim “community health first,” you should be able to show:
- ED access/safety metrics improving (wait, LWBS, boarding)
- chronic disease metrics improving (BP/A1c control, follow-up rates)
- benefits reaching underserved zip codes (stratified reporting)
