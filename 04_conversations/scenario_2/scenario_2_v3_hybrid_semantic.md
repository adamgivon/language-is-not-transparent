## What “community health first” actually means in this choice

### 1) It is not “prevention always wins”
Community health first does **not** mean prioritizing upstream care at the expense of acute access. In a community, **ED timeliness and safety are themselves population health interventions**: delayed emergency care increases mortality, disability, and mistrust—disproportionately for underserved patients who rely on the ED.

**Community health first = protect people from preventable harm across the whole continuum** (acute, chronic, and social access barriers).

---

### 2) It means optimizing *total preventable suffering*, not optimizing one site of care
A useful operational definition:

> Choose investments that produce the greatest reduction in **preventable death, disability, and avoidable crisis utilization**, weighted toward those with the highest burden and least access, within the system’s financial viability constraints.

That forces you to compare ED minutes saved (and harms avoided) against A1c/BP control gains (and complications avoided) using a common goal: preventable harm.

---

### 3) It requires two non-negotiable floors (“do no collapse” guardrails)
A “community health first” stance sets minimum acceptable performance thresholds in both domains:

**Floor A: Acute-care safety/access floor**
- No unsafe ED crowding conditions (e.g., unacceptably high LWBS, excessive boarding, ambulance diversion, repeated safety events).
- Workforce stability sufficient to deliver safe care.
- Accreditation readiness.

**Floor B: Chronic disease/quality floor**
- Avoid CMS penalties by improving the chronic metrics that trigger warning status.
- Demonstrable improvement in high-burden conditions for underserved groups (diabetes, HTN), not just more visits.

If either floor is missed, community health worsens—either immediately (ED) or predictably (chronic disease/penalties that reduce services).

---

### 4) It means weighting benefits toward underserved populations *and* the highest marginal impact
Not all “health gains” are equal. Community health first explicitly prioritizes:
- **high-risk cohorts** (frequent ED users with uncontrolled chronic disease, dual-eligibles, unmanaged hypertension),
- **barrier removal** (access points, hours, transportation, CHWs, medication adherence),
- **interventions with high marginal impact per dollar** (e.g., BP control programs can reduce strokes/MI substantially).

This is how you avoid the trap of spending on what’s visible rather than what changes outcomes.

---

### 5) It treats financial sustainability as a health intervention (because collapse harms the community)
For a nonprofit safety-net-like system, solvency isn’t “secondary”; it’s a condition for serving anyone next year. So “community health first” includes:
- avoiding predictable penalties,
- avoiding workforce-driven service degradation,
- preserving margin to keep services open.

If prevention is chosen, it must be designed to improve metrics fast enough to protect revenue. If ED expansion is chosen, it must not deepen structural drivers of avoidable utilization.

---

### 6) Practically, it implies a “both/and” portfolio—sequenced by deadlines and harm

- **Immediate harm reduction** (ED throughput/safety where patients are currently suffering),
- **Near-term metric protection** (chronic measures to avoid penalties within 18 months),
