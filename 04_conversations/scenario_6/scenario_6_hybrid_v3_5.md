Insurance is just a payment mechanism. **Liability usually exists either way** (negligence/property damage). What changes without insurance is **enforcement**: instead of an insurer that must respond on timelines, you’re facing a private person who can delay, deny, or claim inability to pay.

Below are the main **legal options** that exist in most European legal systems; the names/procedures differ by country.

## 1) Civil claim for damages (the core legal route)
You sue the neighbor (as an individual, and sometimes also the farm business entity if one exists) for **monetary damages**.

Typical heads of claim:
- **Cost of repair** to restore the house (quotes/engineer report support this).
- **Emergency mitigation costs** (tarps, shoring, temporary sealing).
- **Consequential losses** caused by the damage:
  - temporary accommodation (your €80/day),
  - reasonable extra travel/transport,
  - expert fees (engineer/surveyor),
  - possibly “loss of use/enjoyment” depending on country.

What you must prove:
- He caused it (admission + evidence).
- The scope/cost of damage (engineer + quotes).
- Your extra costs are necessary and reasonable (invoices + effort to find reasonable alternatives).

## 2) “Pay now or else” settlement that becomes enforceable (often fastest)
Because court can take time, many cases resolve via a **written settlement** that is enforceable like a judgment (country-dependent but commonly possible through a notary/court-approved settlement).

Key terms you want in writing:
- total amount (repairs + accommodation + experts),
- who selects contractor and by when,
- deadlines/milestones,
- what happens if delays occur,
- payment plan if needed.

**Critical upgrade:** add **security** so it’s not just a promise:
- a registered charge/lien/mortgage over land/property,
- a guarantor,
- assignment of subsidies/receivables (sometimes possible),
- consent to immediate enforcement if he defaults (available in some places via notarization).

If he truly “can’t pay,” security is what turns that into a workable plan rather than endless stalling.

## 3) Court-ordered interim/provisional measures (to stop the bleeding)
Many jurisdictions allow some form of **urgent/interim relief**, for example:
- an order to perform **urgent protective works**,
- an **advance payment** toward undisputed damage,
- measures to preserve evidence (court-appointed expert quickly),
- restrictions that prevent asset dissipation (rare, but possible with strong showing).

Whether you can get these depends heavily on the country and the evidence of urgency (engineer stating uninhabitable/risk of worsening).

## 4) Enforcement against assets if you win (or if you have an enforceable settlement)
This is the part people don’t tell you upfront: **winning “on paper” isn’t the same as collecting**.

If you have a judgment/enforceable deed, you can typically pursue:
- **bank account seizure/garnishment**,
- **wage/income garnishment**,
- **seizure of movable assets** (equipment, vehicles—rules vary),
- **lien/charge on real estate** and potentially forced sale (country-specific thresholds and protections).

If he is genuinely insolvent or highly leveraged, collection can be slow—another reason security early matters.

## 5) Criminal/administrative complaints (sometimes useful, sometimes risky)
Depending on country, using an uninsured/non-road-legal vehicle and causing property damage may trigger:

- municipal/building authority involvement (safety),
- administrative fines.

These routes **don’t usually pay your repair bill directly**, but they can create pressure to settle. They can also escalate local hostility. This is strategy-heavy and country-specific.
