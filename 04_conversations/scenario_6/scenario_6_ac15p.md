## Your core legal position (in most European civil-law systems, and many common-law ones)
- **If he negligently caused physical damage to your property, he is personally liable** for the foreseeable losses that flow from it (repairs, emergency measures, and often reasonable temporary accommodation).
- **Insurance is not what creates liability.** Insurance is just a mechanism that (a) pays quickly, (b) negotiates, and (c) is easier to collect from. Without it, you’re still “right,” but you may have to **prove, sue, and enforce** yourself.

## Your “actual legal options” (generic map; exact names vary by country)

### 1) Formal demand + settlement (fastest if he cooperates)
You send a written demand (registered mail) setting out:
- facts + his admission,
- repair estimates and immediate stabilization needs,
- your temporary housing costs,
- a deadline to agree on a plan.

**Best-practice settlement terms when there’s no insurer:**
- payments made **directly to the contractor**,
- **advance payment** for emergency works now,
- monthly payment of accommodation (or provision of alternate housing),
- a signed schedule to completion,
- **security**: lien/charge on land, guarantor, or notarized enforceable deed.

### 2) Mediation/conciliation (useful, but only with enforceable output)
A mediator can help overcome “I can’t pay” deadlock, but insist on:
- a **written, signed agreement** with dates and consequences,
- ideally notarized/approved so it can be enforced like a judgment (country-dependent).

### 3) Civil lawsuit for damages (standard route)
You sue him for:
- **cost of repair** (often based on expert assessment/quotes),
- **emergency mitigation costs**,
- **consequential losses** (commonly: reasonable temporary accommodation, sometimes travel and related expenses),
- potentially **interest** and legal costs (varies).

Courts often require:
- proof of causation (photos, witness, his admission),
- proof of amounts (quotes, invoices),
- sometimes an independent **court expert**.

### 4) Urgent/interim court measures (because you can’t wait 5 months)
Many jurisdictions allow some form of:
- an order compelling **stabilization/weatherproofing**,
- an **interim payment/advance** when liability is clear and delay causes serious harm,
- preservation-of-evidence orders (court expert quickly).

This is where a local lawyer can be especially valuable, because procedure and thresholds are very country-specific.

### 5) Enforcement against assets (the part people underestimate)
Even with a judgment, you may need to **collect**, e.g.:
- wage/bank account garnishment,
- seizure of assets,
- registration of a lien/charge on property,
- forced sale in extreme cases.

If he truly has limited assets/income, the result can be a **payment plan imposed/structured through enforcement**—but it can still be slow.

### 6) Insolvency/bankruptcy reality
If he’s genuinely insolvent, a court judgment may confirm your claim but still not produce cash quickly. That’s why **security early** (lien/charge/guarantor) matters if there’s any property/land.

### 7) Criminal/administrative routes (sometimes, but not always)
- Pure negligence causing property damage is often **civil**, not criminal, unless there was recklessness, intoxication, or specific statutory breaches.
- There may be administrative issues around using an uninsured/non-road-legal vehicle, but even if authorities act, it usually **doesn’t pay your repairs**.
